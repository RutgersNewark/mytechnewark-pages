Using Zoom Breakout Rooms to Improve Student Engagement

How To Engage Your Students Zoom · Rutgers Canvas

Zoom breakout rooms allow instructors to divide a class meeting into smaller groups so students can discuss ideas, solve problems, collaborate on activities, or work through course material together.

Breakout rooms work best when they are designed as structured learning activities rather than informal discussion spaces. Clear instructions, short time limits, group roles, and a specific task help students stay focused and make the activity more productive.

When to Use Each Tool Use Zoom breakout rooms when students need to talk, compare, collaborate, practice, or produce something together. Use tools such as EchoPoll when you need real-time polling or quick checks for understanding, and use Canvas Discussions when students need more time for asynchronous reflection.

When to Use Breakout Rooms

Use breakout rooms when students need to work together in smaller groups during a live class session. Breakout rooms are most effective when students have a clear task, a short time limit, and something specific to bring back to the main room.

💬
Discussion and Comparison

Send students to small groups to discuss a reading, case, problem, or prompt before returning to full-class conversation.

  • Discussing a reading, case, problem, or prompt
  • Comparing answers before a full-class discussion
  • Building community in an online or hybrid course
🤝
Collaboration and Production

Use breakout rooms when groups need to solve problems together, create something, or prepare a response to share with the class.

  • Solving problems collaboratively
  • Working on a shared document, slide, or worksheet
  • Preparing a short report-out to the full class
✍️
Peer Feedback and Review

Breakout rooms give students a structured space to share and respond to each other's work before submitting assignments or preparing presentations.

  • Giving peer feedback on drafts or presentations
  • Reviewing and comparing each other's work

Before You Open Breakout Rooms

Before sending students into breakout rooms, take a few minutes to prepare the activity. A small amount of structure before opening rooms can prevent confusion once students are separated from the main meeting.

Planning Checklist

  • Decide the purpose of the activity.
  • Choose the group size.
  • Prepare written instructions.
  • Decide whether groups will be assigned automatically, manually, or by student choice.
  • Create or share any documents, slides, worksheets, or links students will need.
  • Decide whether students will have roles — facilitator, note-taker, timekeeper, or reporter.
  • Tell students how long they will have.
  • Explain what groups should produce or report back.
  • Tell students how to ask for help while they are in rooms.

Design Breakout Rooms for Engagement

Start with a Clear Purpose

Tell students why they are going into breakout rooms and how the activity connects to the course goals.

Examples
  • "Use this time to compare your answers before we discuss the problem as a class."
  • "Each group will analyze one part of the case and report back with one recommendation."
  • "Use the shared document to identify two questions you still have about the reading."

Give Specific Tasks and Time Limits

Avoid sending students into breakout rooms with only a general instruction such as "discuss this." Instead, give a focused prompt and a clear deliverable. For most activities, 5–15 minutes is enough time. Shorter time limits often keep groups more focused.

Examples
  • Identify three key points from the reading.
  • Solve problem two and be ready to explain your process.
  • Add one question and one example to the shared document.
  • Choose one spokesperson to summarize your group's recommendation.

Assign Roles When Helpful

Group roles can make participation more equitable and reduce confusion. Roles are especially helpful for longer activities, larger groups, or groups that need to produce something together.

  • Facilitator — Keeps the group focused on the task.
  • Note-taker — Records the group's ideas.
  • Timekeeper — Watches the clock and helps the group finish.
  • Reporter — Shares the group's response when everyone returns.

Make Instructions Available Inside the Rooms

Students may not be able to see your shared screen once they enter breakout rooms. Put instructions somewhere they can access while working. Good options include Zoom chat, a Canvas page, a shared Google Doc, a shared slide deck, or a downloadable worksheet. Written instructions help students stay on task and reduce repeated questions.

Ask Groups to Produce Something

Breakout rooms are more effective when students create a small artifact or prepare a response. Visible outputs make the activity easier to debrief and give students a reason to participate.

  • Notes in a shared document
  • One slide per group
  • A list of questions
  • A short solution or recommendation
  • A report-out statement

Debrief After Rooms Close

After students return to the main room, connect the breakout activity back to the larger class. The debrief is where the activity becomes part of the learning arc, not just a side activity.

Debrief Questions
  • "What did your group notice?"
  • "What was difficult or unclear?"
  • "Which solution did your group choose and why?"
  • "What question should we discuss as a class?"
  • "What pattern did you see across the examples?"

Create Breakout Rooms in Zoom

Use these basic steps to create breakout rooms during a Zoom meeting.

  1. 1
    Start your Zoom meeting.
  2. 2
    Click Breakout Rooms in the meeting toolbar.
  3. 3
    Choose the number of rooms.
  4. 4
    Choose how students will be assigned:
    Automatically (Zoom assigns students), Manually (you choose), or Let participants choose room (students self-select).
  5. 5
    Click Create and review the room assignments.
  6. 6
    Click Open All Rooms when you are ready to begin.
  7. 7
    Use Broadcast Message to send reminders, clarifications, or time warnings to all groups.
  8. 8
    Visit rooms as needed to answer questions or observe progress.
  9. 9
    Click Close All Rooms to bring students back to the main room. Zoom will give students a short countdown before returning them.

Example Breakout Room Activities

These structures work across a wide range of disciplines and course formats. Choose the one that fits your learning objective and available time.

Think-Pair-Share

Students think individually, then compare responses in breakout rooms of two or three. Several groups share with the full class when rooms close.

  • Starting discussion
  • Checking understanding
  • Encouraging broader participation
Case or Scenario Analysis

Each group receives a short case, scenario, or problem. They identify key issues, propose a solution, and report back.

  • Applied learning
  • Professional programs
  • Policy, ethics, business, law, health, and social science
Jigsaw Activity

Each group is assigned a different reading section, dataset, or concept. Groups become responsible for one piece, then share back with the class.

  • Longer readings or complex topics
  • Comparative analysis
  • Student-led explanation
Peer Feedback

Students share draft ideas, outlines, or project plans in small groups using a simple feedback structure: one strength, one question, one suggestion.

  • Writing assignments
  • Presentations and research projects
  • Design or project-based work
Collaborative Problem-Solving

Each group solves a problem and records their steps or reasoning in a shared document.

  • Quantitative and methods courses
  • Lab preparation
  • Exam review
Concept Mapping

Students map relationships among ideas, terms, theories, or events using a shared document, whiteboard, or slide.

  • Review sessions
  • Complex frameworks
  • Connecting course themes

Accessibility and Inclusion

Breakout rooms should give students more ways to participate, not fewer. Plan activities so students can contribute in different ways.

  • Provide written instructions before opening rooms.
  • Allow students to participate through voice, chat, or shared documents.
  • Avoid requiring cameras unless there is a clear instructional reason.
  • Keep tasks focused and time-limited.
  • Give students a way to ask for help.
  • Make expectations for respectful participation clear.
  • Use roles to reduce uneven participation.
  • Consider smaller groups for students who may be hesitant to speak in larger discussions.
Hybrid Sessions For hybrid sessions, plan carefully so remote and in-room students can hear each other, access the same instructions, and participate equitably.

Troubleshooting Tips

Students are quiet in breakout rooms

Try smaller groups, clearer prompts, assigned roles, or a shared document where students must record an answer. Students are more likely to participate when the task is specific and the group has something to produce.

Students are confused about what to do

Post instructions in Zoom chat, Canvas, or a shared document before opening rooms. Repeat the task verbally and include the time limit.

One student does all the talking

Use roles or structured turn-taking. For example, ask each student to share one idea before the group chooses a final response.

Students finish too quickly

Have an extension prompt ready.

  • "What is one counterargument?"
  • "What question would you ask another group?"
  • "How would this change in a different context?"
  • "What evidence supports your answer?"
Students need help while in rooms

Tell students they can use the Ask for Help option in Zoom. You can also visit rooms periodically and use Broadcast Message to send clarifications to all groups.

Breakout rooms are difficult in a hybrid class

Hybrid breakout rooms require extra planning. Consider whether remote students should work together in Zoom rooms while in-person students work in physical groups, or whether each group needs a shared device and a clear audio plan.

Best Practices

👥
Keep Groups Small

Groups of three to five students usually work well. Pairs can be useful for quick activities, while larger groups may need stronger roles and structure.

⏱️
Keep Activities Short

Short, focused breakout activities often work better than long open-ended sessions. Start with 5–10 minutes and adjust based on the activity.

📄
Use Shared Workspaces

Shared documents, slides, or worksheets make group work visible and easier to review. They also give quieter students another way to contribute.

👁️
Visit Rooms Strategically

You do not need to visit every room every time. Drop into a few rooms to listen, answer questions, or identify themes to bring back to the full class.

🔁
Connect Breakout Work to the Main Discussion

Always bring the activity back to the full class. Ask groups to report out, compare responses, identify questions, or connect their work to the next part of the lesson.
