Best Practices for Synchronous Hybrid Class Sessions

Best Practice Teach in the Classroom Hybrid Teaching Zoom Synchronous Remote Students

Synchronous hybrid class sessions include both in-person students and remote students participating at the same time. These sessions work best when remote students are intentionally included in the real-time class experience, rather than treated as observers.

Use the practices below to plan and facilitate a live class session that supports participation, clarity, and engagement for all students.

Before Class

Arrive early and test the room technology

Plan to arrive early enough to test the classroom technology before students join. Check the camera, microphone, speakers, display, and screen sharing.

If you are using Zoom or Microsoft Teams, open the meeting early and confirm that remote students can clearly see and hear the classroom.

Share the agenda in advance

Post or share a brief agenda before class so students know what to expect. Include the main topics, planned activities, and any materials students should have ready.

A simple agenda helps remote students follow the flow of the session and prepare to participate.

Move longer lecture content outside the live session when possible

If possible, pre-record longer lecture segments or provide them before class. Use the live session for discussion, Q&A, problem-solving, demonstrations, group activities, and other forms of active learning.

This helps make the synchronous session more interactive and reduces the risk that remote students become passive viewers.

During Class

Welcome both in-person and remote students

Begin the session by greeting students in the room and students joining remotely. When appropriate, acknowledge remote participants by name so they know they are part of the class community.

"Good morning, everyone in the room, and welcome to those joining us on Zoom. I see Maya, Jordan, and Priya online. We'll make sure to bring both groups into today's discussion."

Slow the pace and pause often

Hybrid sessions usually need a slightly slower pace than fully in-person classes. Pause after asking questions, changing topics, or giving instructions so remote students have time to process and respond.

This is especially important when students may be using chat, waiting to unmute, or following along with shared materials.

Make transitions explicit

Remote students do not always see the same physical cues as students in the room. When changing activities, explain what is happening and what students should do next.

"We're moving from the lecture portion into small-group discussion. In-person students will work with the people near them, and remote students will be placed into breakout rooms."

Use active-learning activities

Build in opportunities for students to do something during the live session. Useful options include:

  • Polls or quick checks for understanding
  • Brief discussion prompts
  • Breakout rooms
  • Paired or small-group work
  • Short case questions
  • Shared document activities
  • Problem-solving exercises

Active learning helps both remote and in-person students stay engaged and gives you more opportunities to check understanding.

Monitor chat and repeat questions

Remote students may use chat to ask questions, respond to prompts, or flag technical issues. Monitor chat throughout the session, or designate a class monitor when possible.

Repeat questions or comments from students in the room before answering them so remote students can follow the conversation.

"Alex asked whether this policy applies to graduate students as well. The answer is…"

Call on remote students directly

Invite remote students into the conversation by name when appropriate. After calling on a remote student, allow extra time for them to unmute or type a response before moving on.

This small pause can make the difference between inclusion and invisibility.

Build in short breaks

For longer sessions, include short breaks so students can reset. A useful guideline is to provide a brief break during extended learning activities, such as about five minutes after every 30 minutes of sustained activity.

Breaks are especially helpful in hybrid sessions because students are managing multiple modes of attention.

Technology and Facilitation Practices

Prioritize audio quality

Audio is the most important part of the remote student experience. If students cannot hear the instructor, classmates, or classroom discussion clearly, they cannot fully participate.

Use the room microphone system when available, repeat in-room comments, and check periodically that remote students can still hear clearly.

Use the camera intentionally

Position or use the camera so remote students can follow the most important part of the session. Depending on the activity, this may include the instructor, whiteboard, classroom display, demonstration materials, or student presenters.

If you are writing on a board, confirm that the writing is visible to remote students. When possible, use a digital whiteboard, document camera, shared slide deck, or posted notes so remote students can access the same information.

Have a backup plan

Technology issues can happen. Prepare a simple contingency plan in advance. Examples include:

  • Continuing discussion through chat
  • Moving an activity into a shared document
  • Posting instructions or updates in Canvas
  • Recording a follow-up explanation after class
  • Sharing slides, notes, or materials after the session

A backup plan helps reduce disruption if the camera, microphone, Zoom, Teams, or classroom display does not work as expected.

Use a class monitor when possible

When available, ask a teaching assistant, co-instructor, or student volunteer to help monitor chat, watch for raised hands, and flag questions from remote students.

This allows the instructor to focus on teaching while still keeping remote participants connected.

Engagement Tips

Mix whole-class and small-group interaction

Use a blend of full-class discussion and smaller group activities. This gives students more ways to participate and prevents the session from becoming instructor-centered.

When using small groups, be clear about how remote and in-person students will participate. You may use breakout rooms for remote students, in-room pairs for students physically present, or mixed groups when the technology and activity support it.

Offer multiple ways to participate

Use prompts that students can answer verbally, in chat, through a poll, or in a shared document. Multiple participation options are especially helpful for remote students who may have audio issues, bandwidth limitations, or less confidence interrupting a live room discussion.

Encourage student-to-student interaction

Design activities where students interact with each other, not only with the instructor. Ask students to respond to classmates, collaborate on a shared task, or compare solutions across groups.

Hybrid sessions are more effective when remote and in-person students both have meaningful roles in the learning community.

Quick Checklist

Before and during each synchronous hybrid class session:

  • Start the Zoom or Teams session early
  • Test the camera, microphone, speakers, and screen sharing
  • Confirm remote students can see and hear the classroom
  • Share a brief agenda
  • Use active learning instead of long lecture
  • Watch chat and include remote students by name
  • Repeat in-room questions for remote students
  • Use clear transitions between activities
  • Build in short breaks during longer sessions
  • Have a backup plan for technology issues
Key Takeaway

Successful synchronous hybrid teaching requires intentional facilitation. Remote students should be able to hear, see, participate, ask questions, collaborate, and contribute to the class in real time. A little extra structure before and during class can make the session feel more connected for everyone.
